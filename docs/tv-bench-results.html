Google TV vs Fire TV Benchmarks

Streaming a 4K60 test clip from the PC to each TV three ways: stock WebRTC (Chromium to the TV's WebRTC library), WebRTC tuned for cloud gaming (NVENC frames carried by Chromium's WebRTC, low-latency receive and direct decode on the TV), and a native NVENC → RTP → low-latency MediaCodec path. Latency is from capture on the PC to the frame decoded on the TV, using synchronized clocks; "on screen" adds the TV's display pipeline up to its HDMI output.

Stock WebRTC vs cloud-gaming WebRTC vs native (60 fps, H.264)

Capture → decoded, median, log scale. Pre-encoded: the PC streams frames it encoded beforehand, timestamped when sent, so the number is transfer, receive and decode alone.

Cloud gaming cuts WebRTC latency about tenfold (117–352 ms down to 12–31 ms) and holds 60 fps up to 4K, where stock WebRTC drops as low as 36 fps on the Google TV. It stays 5–7 ms behind the native path at 1080p: a few ms to hand each frame into Chromium's sender, the rest in the TV's WebRTC receive. Pre-encoded takes encoding out: 12.5–14.2 ms at 1080p over WebRTC and 8.7–9.6 ms native, i.e. transfer plus decode.

Screen mirroring: PC screen → TV screen (1080p60)

Measured on the TV's own screen: the PC shows a frame-numbered pattern and the TV app captures its screen and times when each frame appears. Includes PC capture, encode, Wi-Fi, decode and the TV compositor.

Cloud-gaming mirroring halves the delay of stock WebRTC on both TVs and is steadier (p95 within 2–7 ms of the median). Miracast is not measured yet: Fire TV's Miracast receiver takes the TV off Wi-Fi while it runs, so the app now times it offline and collects the results when the TV reconnects; the run needs mirroring started by hand (Win+K). The Google TV has no Miracast receiver.

Native path latency at 60 fps

Capture → decoded, median, after tuning. Bars per codec and TV.

Fire TV decodes faster: its hardware decoder needs about 4 ms per 4K frame, against about 12 ms on the Google TV. H.265 beats H.264 at 4K on both, mostly because the PC encodes it in 2.2 ms instead of 6–7 ms.

Where the time goes on the Google TV (60 fps, native)

Capture → decoded, plus the wait in the TV's display pipeline until the frame is on its HDMI output.

The display pipeline is the biggest piece: Android's compositor needs frames about 17.5 ms before the refresh that shows them, so 22–25 ms passes between decode and screen at every resolution. Fire OS doesn't report real display times, so this view only exists for the Google TV.

Vsync pacing on the Google TV

Same tests, PC frame clock free-running versus locked to the TV's display by the pacer. 60 fps, native.

Free-running is a lottery: depending on where the frame phase lands, the same setup shows 28–50 ms and as few as 51 fps on screen. Locked, it holds 59.4–60.3 fps shown with consistent latency. The one locked dip (H.265 1440p, 55.6 fps) was Wi-Fi packet loss.

Google TV tops out around 40–57 fps at 60 fps targets even though its decoder is fast enough; the limit is Android's WebRTC receive path. VP8 collapses above 1080p. Fire TV holds about 59 fps up to 1440p60, but VP8 above 1080p and AV1 at 1440p+ produce no video at all. At 30 fps both TVs mostly pass up to 4K.

Before and after tuning (native, 60 fps)

Capture → decoded, median. Before: NVENC preset P3, old receiver. After: preset P1, three-way H.265 split encode, decoder backlog fix, vsync pacing.

Fire TV's H.264 decoder had been holding 2–4 frames in some tests (43–79 ms); that is gone. On the Google TV the gains are at 1440p and 4K, from faster encoding: 4K H.264 dropped from 28.8 to 23.5 ms and 4K H.265 from 23.2 to 18.5 ms.
